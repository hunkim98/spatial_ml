\title{What Makes a Map Segmentation Task Transferable?\\
A Diagnostic and Ablation of Synthetic Pretraining on USGS Maps}

\author{Hun Kim}
\affiliation{%
  \institution{Harvard University}
  \city{Cambridge}

\email{donghunkim@mde.harvard.edu}

\begin{abstract}
What makes a raster-map segmentation task transferable from one domain to
another by a fine-tunable model, and what bounds its success? We study this
on legend-keyed polygon extraction---finding all pixels in a map matching a
queried legend swatch---on the DARPA Critical Mineral Assessment (CMA)
benchmark, the standard evaluation set for this task. Prior supervised
methods on this benchmark typically require 14+ hand-annotated USGS maps.
We ask whether a generic synthetic prior can substitute for most of that
annotation, and characterize which properties of the benchmark itself bound
any color-based method's success.
Our pipeline renders ${\sim}27{,}000$
$(\textsc{image},\textsc{pattern},\textsc{mask})$ triplets from 219 US municipal
zoning GeoJSONs with 8 randomized rendering dimensions, at zero annotation
cost, and trains a FiLM-conditioned U-Net to perform legend-keyed segmentation.
On the DARPA CMA validation set the zero-shot model achieves only median F1
$0.016$, a gap we trace to a structural property of the benchmark:
\textbf{98\% of USGS legend swatches have a CIEDE2000 distance below 15 to
the nearest other swatch in the same map}. Within this structurally adversarial
regime, fine-tuning the pretrained model on just two USGS maps yields
measurable held-out improvement on the remaining 22 (mean F1 $0.126$ vs
$0.086$ zero-shot; paired bootstrap $\Delta = +0.040$, 95\% CI
$[+0.031, +0.050]$, $P{=}1.000$), with every map showing positive gain. A
randomly-initialized network trained on the same two maps with identical
hyperparameters achieves only $0.017$ mean F1 ($7.55\times$ lower, CI
$[6.78, 8.50]$). Under a fair higher learning rate that lets random init
actually learn, the scratch baseline reaches $0.083$; pretraining still wins
by $1.52\times$ (paired $\Delta = +0.043$), isolating the contribution of the
synthetic prior to the encoder representation.
We further trace the prior's gain across $N{=}1, 2, 5, 10$ fine-tune maps,
producing an annotation-efficiency curve that anchors the transferability
claim empirically.
Our absolute numbers remain below prior fully-supervised
results~\cite{luo2023icm, lin2023loam} on this benchmark; we argue that
map-segmentation transferability has two distinguishable components---a
prior contribution we \emph{measure} and a benchmark ceiling we
\emph{characterize}---and that progress depends on composing the prior with
disambiguation machinery adequate for the swatch-confusability regime.
Code and pipeline: \url{https://github.com/hunkim98/spatial_ml}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Historical raster maps---geological surveys, zoning records, cadastral
plans---contain extensive geospatial information that remains locked in
image form. Digitizing them into vector data underpins applications from
critical-mineral assessment to urban-history research~\cite{chiang2014survey}.
The core operation is \emph{legend-keyed segmentation}: given a map image and
a legend swatch, return a binary mask of pixels matching that legend entry.

Recent supervised systems on the DARPA Critical Mineral Assessment (CMA)
benchmark approach this with hand-engineered preprocessing pipelines and 14+
manually annotated USGS maps. The ICM team's 6-channel U-Net~\cite{luo2023icm}
took first place in the 2022 challenge with OCR-based text matching; the
LOAM system~\cite{lin2023loam} extended this to a 7-channel preprocessing
stack with shuffle attention, achieving median F1 $0.809$. Concurrent work
includes a USGS-deployed modular pipeline that also incorporates synthetic
data~\cite{duan2025digmapper}, foundation-model linear probing for few-shot
historical map segmentation~\cite{sterzinger2025fewshot}, and synthetic
bootstrapping with degradation modeling~\cite{arzoumanidis2025synthetic}.

We ask: \emph{what determines whether this segmentation task is transferable
from one map domain to another by a fine-tunable model?} Two ingredients
plausibly matter: the quality of the pretrained encoder representation
(does it carry features that transfer at all?) and the structural difficulty
of the target benchmark (is there a ceiling no amount of fine-tuning can
exceed without additional machinery?). We measure both. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{quantitative diagnosis of the DARPA CMA benchmark's
      structural difficulty}: 98\% of legend swatches have a CIEDE2000
      distance ${<}15$ to the nearest other swatch in the same map. Per-feature
      F1 correlates significantly with both color distinctiveness and feature
      area, but the dominant regime is one of confusable colors.

    \item A \textbf{controlled head-to-head ablation} of synthetic-pretraining
      contribution under matched 2-map fine-tune protocols. With identical
      hyperparameters, pretrained$+$fine-tune beats random$+$fine-tune by
      $7.55\times$ at the protocol's learning rate and by $1.52\times$ at a
      learning rate favorable to random init---isolating the gain to feature
      transfer rather than to the fine-tune procedure.

    \item A \textbf{paired-bootstrap evaluation framework} reporting CIs on
      every per-feature delta on the full 22-map held-out set. To our knowledge
      the first such isolation of the pretraining contribution on this
      benchmark.
\end{enumerate}

We do \emph{not} claim parity with the supervised state of the art. The
absolute numbers (mean F1 ${\sim}0.13$) remain far below LOAM. The takeaway
is that transferability has two distinct components---a pretrained prior
contribution we can measure (and is real) and a task-intrinsic ceiling we
can characterize (and is dominated by swatch confusability). Future work on
the prior contribution would need to compose with disambiguation machinery
to push absolute numbers; future benchmark design that wants to reward
transferable models should target maps whose hardness is not bounded by
this single property.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Polygon extraction from raster maps has a long history~\cite{chiang2014survey,
liu2019topomap}. The DARPA CMA challenge~\cite{goldman2023cma} produced the
first deep-learning systems for legend-keyed extraction from USGS geological
maps: ICM's 6-channel U-Net with OCR text matching~\cite{luo2023icm}, then
LOAM's 7-channel preprocessing stack with shuffle attention and color-set
auxiliary embeddings~\cite{lin2023loam}, which achieved median F1 $0.809$
trained on 14 annotated maps. The DIGMAPPER system~\cite{duan2025digmapper}
extends this line into a USGS-deployed modular pipeline that also incorporates
synthetic data generation. On historical maps more broadly,
recent concurrent work explores few-shot foundation-model linear
probing~\cite{sterzinger2025fewshot} and synthetic-data bootstrapping with
degradation modeling~\cite{arzoumanidis2025synthetic}, both evaluated on
ICDAR/Siegfried datasets~\cite{chazalon2021icdarmapseg} rather than DARPA CMA.
Foundation models for natural images such as SAM~\cite{kirillov2023sam}
provide strong general segmentation but lack the legend-key-conditioned
primitive that map digitization requires.

Our work differs on three axes: (i)~we evaluate transfer from synthetic
\emph{municipal zoning} renderings to real \emph{USGS geological} maps, an
aesthetic gap none of the concurrent works address; (ii)~we retain the
legend-keyed task framing throughout
(unlike~\cite{sterzinger2025fewshot, arzoumanidis2025synthetic} which target
generic semantic segmentation); and (iii)~we isolate the contribution of the
synthetic prior through a matched pretrained-vs-scratch ablation that, to our
knowledge, has not been previously reported on this benchmark.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Synthetic pipeline.} We collect 219 US municipal zoning GeoJSONs
and render each ${\sim}20$ times with randomized values across 8 dimensions:
fill color (HSV space), hatching (10 Esri styles, $p{=}0.59$), rotation
(0--360\textdegree), page-size scale (8 presets), basemap (CartoDB Positron /
Voyager / none), zone-label rendering ($p{=}0.91$), aged-paper effects
($p{=}0.31$, including yellowing, fold lines, vignettes), and boundary blur.
Per-zone $32{\times}32$ pattern thumbnails are extracted from the polygon's
\emph{pole of inaccessibility} with a 30\% safety margin to avoid boundary
contamination. Binary masks are rasterized at full resolution. After
filtering zones with ${<}0.5\%$ pixel coverage and a source-aware split,
we obtain $27{,}071$ training and $1{,}307$ validation triplets with no
manual annotation.

\paragraph{Architecture.} A FiLM-conditioned vanilla U-Net (37.9M parameters):
4-level encoder with $2\times$ max-pool downsampling per level
($3{\to}64{\to}128{\to}256{\to}512$, 1024-channel bottleneck), pattern-thumbnail
encoder ($3{\to}256$ via three stride-2 convs $+$ adaptive avg-pool), and a
4-level decoder with FiLM modulation~\cite{perez2018film} at each level:
$\mathbf{d}_l = \gamma_l(\mathbf{c}) \odot \mathrm{Conv}(\mathrm{Up}(\mathbf{d}_{l+1}) \,\|\, \mathbf{e}_l) + \beta_l(\mathbf{c})$.

\paragraph{Pretraining.} SGD with momentum $0.999$, learning rate $10^{-5}$,
Dice loss, 40 epochs at $512{\times}512$, batch size 4, gradient clipping at
$1.0$, mixed precision. Source-aware splitting prevents geographic
leakage~\cite{lin2023loam}; stratified sampling balances density bins.
The pretrained model achieves IoU $0.574$ / F1 $0.615$ on held-out synthetic
validation maps (no city overlap with training; best checkpoint at epoch 36
of 40), confirming that the synthetic task is learnable and that the resulting
checkpoint is a competent base before USGS fine-tune.
Figure~\ref{fig:training} shows training trajectories for both the base model
on synthetic data (left) and the two-map USGS fine-tunes on top of it (right).

\paragraph{USGS evaluation.} We use the DARPA CMA validation set (24 maps with
ground-truth rasters; 1{,}141 polygon features). Two of the 24 maps
(\textsc{AR\_StJoe}, \textsc{AK\_Dillingham}) are held out as the fine-tune set;
the remaining 22 are evaluated. Inference is tiled at $512{\times}512$ with
$128$-pixel overlap, sigmoid threshold $0.5$. Per-feature F1 is computed
against per-feature GT rasters from the benchmark answer key.

\paragraph{Fine-tune protocol.} 20 epochs on the 2 fine-tune maps, SGD
momentum $0.999$, learning rate $10^{-6}$ (a fine-tuning rate, lower than
pretraining), encoder frozen for the first 3 epochs to prevent catastrophic
forgetting then unfrozen, Dice loss, 64 random tile crops per feature per
epoch, batch size 4. The same protocol is applied to a randomly-initialized
network for the head-to-head baseline.

\section{Diagnostic: Why USGS Map Extraction Is Hard}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

Before reporting the fine-tune comparison, we characterize a structural
property of the benchmark that bounds what \emph{any} method can achieve
without explicit color-disambiguation machinery.

For each of the 1{,}141 evaluated features, we compute the CIEDE2000
distance between its legend swatch (cleaned via inset $+$ median-RGB
denoising) and the nearest other swatch in the same map's legend. Table
\ref{tab:deltae_bins} reports zero-shot F1 binned by this distance.

Mean F1 rises monotonically with color distinctiveness (Spearman $r{=}0.21$,
$p{<}10^{-12}$, $n{=}1{,}141$) and with feature area (Pearson $r{=}0.45$ on
$\log_{10}$ area fraction, $p{<}10^{-56}$). Figure~\ref{fig:deltae} visualizes
the binned F1 trend. The model has the right inductive bias---it succeeds on
individual features up to F1 $0.93$---but the \emph{aggregate} ceiling is
bounded by the fact that 98\% of swatches have a near-twin in their own map.
Distinguishing them requires either explicit disambiguation machinery (LOAM's
color-set auxiliary vectors~\cite{lin2023loam}) or substantial real supervision
in the confusable regime. We adopt neither in the pretrained model; the next
section measures how much fine-tuning on 2 maps recovers.

Pretrained $+$ fine-tune outperforms zero-shot by $+0.040$ mean F1 (95\% CI
$[+0.031, +0.050]$), with $P{=}1.000$ across 2{,}000 paired bootstraps. All
22 held-out maps show positive Δ; the per-map effect ranges from
$+9\%$ on the densest map (DC\_Frederick, 267 features, mean
$\Delta E{=}3.3$) to $+340\%$ on KY\_WestFranklin (9 features,
$\Delta E{=}13.3$).

\subsection{Pretraining vs.\ Random Init}

Under the matched fine-tune protocol (LR=$10^{-6}$), the random-init baseline
collapses to mean F1 $0.017$---essentially the trivial-prediction baseline.
The pretrained vs.\ matched-LR-scratch ratio is $7.55\times$ (CI $[6.78, 8.50]$,
paired $\Delta = +0.109$ CI $[+0.099, +0.120]$, $P{=}1.000$). At this LR a
randomly-initialized encoder cannot escape its initialization in 20 epochs.

This matched-LR comparison is exactly the protocol implied by ``same
hyperparameters,'' but it inflates the apparent advantage. Under a learning
rate favorable to random init (LR=$10^{-4}$, no encoder freeze), the scratch
baseline reaches mean F1 $0.083$, narrowing the gap to $1.52\times$
(paired $\Delta = +0.043$, CI also tight).

The takeaway is that both numbers are honest. The matched-LR result shows
random init cannot exploit the same fine-tune protocol as the pretrained
model---feature transfer is doing real work. The fair-LR result shows the
gap is not an LR-mismatch artifact: pretraining still wins when scratch is
given a more aggressive optimizer setting. The combination isolates the
contribution of the synthetic prior to the encoder representation.

\subsection{Per-Map Consistency}

Across the 22 held-out maps, every map shows pretrained$>$zero-shot (22/22)
and every map shows pretrained$>$matched-LR-scratch (22/22). The gain is
not driven by outliers: removing the largest-improvement map
(KY\_WestFranklin) drops the aggregate mean by only $0.001$.
Figure~\ref{fig:per_map} shows the per-map breakdown.

We also tested fine-tune set composition by replacing AK\_Dillingham (typical
map, $\Delta E{=}6.2$) with AK\_Hughes (the second-hardest in the benchmark,
$\Delta E{=}2.5$). Held-out mean F1 across the 22 remaining maps
($n{=}1{,}040$ features) was $0.120$, within sampling noise of the original
$0.126$ ($n{=}1{,}083$). Fine-tune set composition is robust within the
difficulty spectrum we tested.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What this paper does and does not claim.} We do not claim
parity with prior fully-supervised methods on this benchmark. The
contribution is methodological: an isolated measurement of how much
synthetic pretraining helps, and a quantitative diagnosis of why the
absolute ceiling on this benchmark is low for any color-only method.

\paragraph{Why the absolute numbers are bounded.} The 98\% confusable-color
property (\S\ref{sec:diagnostic}) implies that any color-only method faces
a hard ceiling on this benchmark without explicit disambiguation machinery.
The gap between our pretrained-and-fine-tuned model and prior
fully-supervised approaches is consistent with this ceiling: methods that
explicitly model legend-color sets~\cite{lin2023loam} or text
labels~\cite{luo2023icm} can break the color-only bound, at the cost of
domain-specific machinery. Future work should evaluate whether
color-distinctiveness conditioning can be composed with our synthetic prior.

\paragraph{Concurrent work.} DIGMAPPER~\cite{duan2025digmapper} reports
deploying a USGS-integrated pipeline that includes synthetic-data generation;
our setup differs in being end-to-end-trained, key-conditioned, and ablated
against random init. \cite{arzoumanidis2025synthetic}~studies synthetic
bootstrapping for historical maps via cartographic-style transfer with
degradation modeling, on a different (unnamed) corpus.
\cite{sterzinger2025fewshot}~studies few-shot foundation-model linear
probing on ICDAR-2021/Siegfried, also a different task framing.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i)~Single random seed; we expect seed-variance to be
small relative to the reported deltas (paired bootstrap CIs are tight) but
have not measured it. (ii)~Our scaling sweep stops at $N{=}10$ fine-tune
maps, matching the LOAM-comparable annotation budget; we do not measure
saturation behavior beyond this regime.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We approached legend-keyed map segmentation from the perspective of
\emph{what makes a task transferable for a fine-tunable model}, and
disentangled two answers on the DARPA CMA benchmark. First, synthetic
pretraining provides a measurable, bootstrap-significant transferable
encoder prior: pretrained$+$fine-tune outperforms scratch$+$fine-tune by
$1.52\times$ under a fair learning rate and by $7.55\times$ under matched
hyperparameters, with every held-out map showing positive gain. Second, the
benchmark itself has a structural ceiling---98\% of legend swatches share
near-twins with another swatch in the same map (CIEDE2000 ${<}15$)---which
no color-only method can transcend without disambiguation machinery. The
practical implication is that progress on map-segmentation transferability
depends less on annotation volume and more on (i) composing a transferable
prior with (ii) a mechanism that handles intrinsic task ambiguity.
Benchmarks that conflate these two factors will reward neither.
